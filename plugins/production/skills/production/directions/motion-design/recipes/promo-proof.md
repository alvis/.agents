# Promotional reel recipes: explanation and proof

Read from `directions/motion-design/concept.md` when the chosen recipe is on this shelf.

The promo shelf holds ten recipes for 10 to 30 second promotional reels: R1, R2, R3, R5 and R7 here, R4, R6, R8, R9 and R10 in [desire, humour and identity](directions/motion-design/recipes/promo-identity.md). One reel carries one main promise. Promotion is the purpose, not a required story form: a reel may teach, perform, compare, reveal a collection or show a process without characters or conflict. Every timing is a starting allocation for the runtime it names.

## Shelf rules

- Choose the job first. Explain: R1, R2, R7. Demonstrate: R3, R5. Create desire: R4. Entertain through recognition: R6. Build identity: R8. Invite participation: R9. Make a phrase memorable: R10.
- Every recipe expands hook, body, close; that skeleton is useful, not proven to work equally for every audience or platform.
- Proof is something viewers can reasonably verify from the actual product, service or event, never an invented capability in an animated interface.
- Ask why this explanation, performance, visual experience or story belongs to this offer. A demonstration answers causally; an educational reel makes relevant expertise useful; a teaser makes the actual music desirable; a positioning piece expresses a distinct point of view. A logo on unrelated material is no answer.
- Shorten by removing ideas, not by speeding up actions: keep problem, credible intervention and result, and cut secondary setup, extra examples and additional claims first. A 10 s cut is a smaller complete argument.
- The offer and next action must read in the intended viewing context ([platforms](references/platforms.md)). Caption meaningful speech with reading time. A muted cutdown of a music-led reel must still identify the artist, release or event but need not carry the music's experience. Judge the piece with sound as well as silent.

## Practical-benefit durations

| Length | Opening | Development and proof | Payoff and next action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 s | 0–2 s one clear situation | 2–6 s one action | 6–8 s result; 8–10 s identity, already shown visually |
| 15 s | 0–3 s problem or desire | 3–8 s demonstration | 8–12 s result; 12–15 s invitation |
| 20 s | 0–3 s hook; 3–6 s context | 6–14 s action and proof | 14–17 s benefit; 17–20 s invitation |
| 30 s | 0–3 s hook; 3–8 s context | 8–20 s demonstration with one objection addressed | 20–25 s result; 25–30 s invitation |

These are drafting budgets, not best-performing lengths; a recipe's own timing overrides them. A short closing slot never excuses unreadable copy: cut copy, show identity earlier or lengthen the ending.

## Borrowing other shelves

- Teach one useful thing: E1 or E5 with R1 or R7 ([explainers](directions/motion-design/recipes/explainer-mechanism.md)). For 30 s, 0–3 s useful question; 3–8 s minimum setup; 8–22 s one visible explanation or demonstration; 22–26 s accurate takeaway; 26–30 s identity and invitation. Teach a complete narrow relation, keep the qualification that prevents a false takeaway, and do not make the product the answer to everything.
- Let the track set the structure: [V1, V2 or V4](directions/motion-design/recipes/music-video.md) with R8 or R10. Pick a real 10 to 30 s phrase with a local arrival; the phrase, not the table above, sets visual timing. Establish a visual signature early, develop it, then resolve while the artist, release or brand stays identifiable. Shorten copy or pick another excerpt before cutting across the musical thought.
- Show a process or collection: [X4](directions/motion-design/recipes/essay.md) with R5, or X5 with R8. The order comes from the transformation or comparison, not an invented character problem; keep real conditions when the animation implies a real capability.

## R1 · Problem, Demonstration, Relief

- Use when: a tool or service with a clear practical benefit. Default 15 s.
- Ingredients: one specific frustration; one product action; a visible changed state; one next action.
- Method: open with the frustration already happening and its consequence clear without a paragraph of text → show the product action in a readable state so viewers see what changed, not just a moving cursor → return to the original problem with a narrower, believable improvement → name the product and one next step, with the product recognisable earlier than the last frame.
- Timing: for 15 s, 0–3 s problem; 3–8 s action; 8–12 s result; 12–15 s identity and invitation.
- Variations: a visual metaphor may dramatise the frustration if it connects to a real action; at 30 s deepen the demonstration rather than adding features.
- Fix: if the product magically solves everything, show one named input, one operation and its output.
- Finish test: Can a fresh viewer complete "the product helped (person) do (specific thing)"?

## R2 · Result First, Then How

- Use when: the outcome is more immediately interesting than the process. Default 20 s.
- Ingredients: an appealing result; a clear starting state; one understandable mechanism; continuity between before and after.
- Method: show enough result to spark curiosity without hiding what kind of offer this is → jump back once to the starting state, making the time change clear → show the decisive intervention on the same object or task → return to the result with new understanding and close on product and next action.
- Timing: for 20 s, 0–3 s result; 3–6 s starting state; 6–14 s mechanism; 14–17 s result revisited; 17–20 s invitation.
- Variations: open on the finished object, a user reaction or an unusual configuration; a reaction never substitutes for showing the result.
- Fix: if the product's role is unclear, simplify the chronology and make the intervention explicit.
- Finish test: Does the second appearance of the result answer the opening question?

## R3 · One Claim, One Test

- Use when: a capability people must believe, not just understand. Default 15 s.
- Ingredients: one relevant test; visible conditions; the actual product; a readable outcome.
- Method: state or show a challenge representative of the advertised use → let the product perform without hiding the decisive moment behind decorative cuts → show the outcome under comparable conditions → close on the precise benefit shown and one next action.
- Timing: for 15 s, 0–3 s challenge; 3–9 s test; 9–12 s result; 12–15 s invitation.
- Variations: a contrast, a constraint or a repeatable task; side by side only when conditions are fair and legible.
- Fix: if the test proves the wrong thing, rewrite the claim to match the evidence or pick a relevant test.
- Finish test: What can the viewer conclude from this test, and what can they not?

## R5 · Before, Intervention, After

- Use when: repair, organisation, customisation or another visible transformation. Default 20 s.
- Ingredients: a recognisable starting state; one changed property; a visible intervention; a matched comparison.
- Method: establish the defect, disorder or unmet need → show enough of the intervention to make the result credible → present the after state from a comparable viewpoint, keeping some unchanged detail for continuity → show it in use and the next action.
- Timing: for 20 s, 0–4 s before; 4–11 s intervention; 11–16 s after and use; 16–20 s invitation.
- Variations: a split comparison for immediate proof, a matched return for emotional continuity.
- Fix: if everything changes at once, hold the other conditions constant so the claimed improvement is identifiable.
- Finish test: Can the viewer point to the exact improvement rather than say the second image looks nicer?

## R7 · One Useful Analogy

- Use when: a service or concept that is hard to picture. Default 30 s.
- Ingredients: one unfamiliar idea; one familiar relationship that accurately resembles it; one real-product connection; one limit to the analogy.
- Method: name the audience's problem without a vocabulary lesson → show the familiar relationship doing useful work → map each essential part to the real process, simply and consistently → show a real task or result with the brand's role clear → end on the practical takeaway and invitation, not another metaphor.
- Timing: for 30 s, 0–3 s problem; 3–10 s analogy; 10–21 s mapping and demonstration; 21–26 s result; 26–30 s invitation.
- Variations: sorting, handovers, routes, assembly or collaboration as real relationships, not decorative shapes.
- Fix: if the analogy implies automation or guarantees the product lacks, remove that mapping however good it looks.
- Finish test: Can the viewer explain the actual service without the fictional world?
