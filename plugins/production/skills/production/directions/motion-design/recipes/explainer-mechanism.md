# Explainer recipes: mechanisms

Read from `directions/motion-design/concept.md` when the chosen recipe is on this shelf.

The explainer shelf holds eight learning structures for explanation, demonstration and factual stories: E1 to E3 here, E4 to E8 in [correction, practice and evidence](directions/motion-design/recipes/explainer-practice.md). The change happens in the viewer's understanding; a character is optional. Every timing is a starting allocation for the runtime it names.

## Choose by what the viewer should be able to do

| After watching, the viewer can | Recipe | What drives it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explain why an unexpected result happens | E1 | A question and its answer |
| Trace how a system works | E2 | Transformations and handovers |
| Grasp an unfamiliar abstraction | E3 | A controlled analogy |
| Replace a wrong explanation | E4 | A contradiction settled by evidence |
| Perform a task or solve a similar problem | E5 | Growing learner independence |
| Relate things across sizes or levels | E6 | A guided change of perspective |
| Read a pattern without overclaiming | E7 | Evidence narrowing an explanation |
| Use a concept to make a decision | E8 | An outcome that depends on understanding |

Pick an audience-facing format and a teaching structure together: historical facts, microhistory and origin stories come from [history](directions/motion-design/recipes/history-true.md); what-ifs, visual mathematics, hidden systems, and cutaways from [science mechanisms](directions/motion-design/recipes/science-mechanisms.md); simulations, scale journeys, riddles, and game-videos from [science play](directions/motion-design/recipes/science-play.md). The E recipe supplies the learning structure under either.

Before drafting, complete "After watching, this audience can (explain, distinguish, predict, calculate or perform) one thing" and name the knowledge they already need. "Learn about science" is too broad to organise a short.

## Accuracy rules

- Script in two passes: first the accurate explanation with no jokes, metaphors or music, then how to reveal it. The reveal may change; the argument may not.
- Plan each beat in three columns: the viewer's current model, what picture and words establish, what the viewer can now do. The usual beats are question, mechanism, boundary, transfer.
- Words name and explain; the animation shows the relation the sentence describes. A diagram changes for an explanatory reason, not whenever the narrator names a new noun.
- Introduce unfamiliar labels before parts interact, and keep each label close and stable. Keep captions for access without a competing on-screen essay.
- Every metaphor needs a return ticket: record its correspondences and the false implications it invites. A cute guide may direct attention but must not supply false intention, agency or physics.
- Review pictures as claims: relative size, speed, order, arrows and apparent certainty can mislead while the narration is correct.
- Review sources separately from style; an attractive style does not make a claim correct.
- Music never implies proof: lower musical pressure during reasoning, and give no triumphant cue before the explanation is complete.

## Length and learning check (starting allocations)

- 10 s: one already-understandable observation or action; never label it a complete lesson in a complex concept.
- 20 to 30 s: one question, one visible mechanism, one takeaway, keeping any qualification needed to avoid a false takeaway inside the clip.
- 3 to 5 min: one concept with a worked example, its necessary limitation and a transfer check.
- 6 to 10 min: add contrasting cases, a deeper mechanism or learner practice, not more facts.
- Inserted retrieval questions are worth considering because tests inserted into online lectures improved learning in published experiments; that does not prove any one quiz helps every animation.
- For an educational song, remembering the refrain and understanding the concept are separate checks.

## E1 · Predict, Observe, Explain, Transfer

- Use when: a surprising mechanism, visual puzzle, mathematical idea or experiment.
- Ingredients: one answerable question; a plausible prediction; a visible result; an explanation that accounts for it; a new application.
- Method: show the situation, the conditions needed to predict, and what to predict → give a brief thinking pause, smaller for a feed than a classroom, holding the setup on screen → reveal the result with the relevant conditions visible, not buried under a reaction montage → revisit the event in stages, tying each to the part of the result it explains → change one input or context, ask what happens now, and end on the principle.
- Timing: for 5:00, 0:00–0:30 question; 0:30–1:00 prediction; 1:00–1:30 observation; 1:30–3:45 explanation; 3:45–5:00 transfer and recap.
- Variations: a puzzle makes the audience solve, an experiment makes them predict, a construction makes them discover a relation.
- Fix: if a surprising fact ends the film, add the mechanism and one fresh application.
- Finish test: Can the viewer use the explanation on the changed case rather than repeat the reveal?

## E2 · Follow One Thing Through a System

- Use when: a manufacturing process, service, biological mechanism or technical workflow.
- Ingredients: one traceable unit; an entry and an exit; meaningful stages; a stable system map; a boundary to the explanation.
- Method: establish what enters and the question its journey answers → show a simple overview before the first stage, keeping a locator available when orientation is hard → at each stage answer what arrives, what happens, why, and what leaves → include a branch, delay or feedback path only when essential, and mark when one example is not representative → return to the overview and tie the final state to the input.
- Timing: for 6:00, 0:00–0:35 question and input; 0:35–1:00 overview; 1:00–4:40 selected stages; 4:40–5:20 branch or limitation; 5:20–6:00 whole-system return.
- Variations: follow material, information, responsibility or an order; show a cycle when the system cycles. A recurring handover sound must not make an unproven route seem automatic.
- Fix: if a mascot tours unrelated facts, make every stage transform the same tracked thing.
- Finish test: Could the viewer sketch the stages and explain each handover?

## E3 · Familiar Model, Real Mechanism, Boundary

- Use when: an abstract concept that a familiar relationship can carry.
- Ingredients: the real concept; one useful analogy; explicit correspondences; the point where the analogy breaks; a literal return.
- Method: state the real question before the metaphor → demonstrate a familiar relationship that keeps the relevant logic → map elements to the real concept one at a time, showing analogy and real model together with stable labels → explain one consequential limit and strip decoration that teaches a false cause, scale or intention → return to the real representation and apply the concept without the metaphor.
- Timing: for 4:00, 0:00–0:25 real question; 0:25–1:15 familiar model; 1:15–2:35 mapping; 2:35–3:10 boundary; 3:10–4:00 literal application.
- Variations: journeys, sharing, storage, routes or assembly, only when the relationship truly fits; not every abstraction improves with anthropomorphism.
- Fix: if viewers remember the characters but not the concept, make the real mapping visible and end outside the metaphor.
- Finish test: Can a learner say both "this part corresponds to" and "this part does not"?
