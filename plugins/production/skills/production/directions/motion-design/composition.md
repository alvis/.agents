# Composition recipes

Read from `directions/motion-design/build.md` when laying out a shot.

Before deciding how anything moves, decide what the viewer must understand first, second and last. Pick the recipe by the shot's job; none prescribes a renderer. The source gives no timings: a shot's length comes from the storyboard's bar grid and the pacing rules in [build](directions/motion-design/build.md).

## C1 · One lead, supporting context
- Use when: a product action, visual comedy, a character's choice, or any overloaded shot.
- Ingredients: one decisive action; only the context needed to read it; optional background activity.
- Method: name the first read → make the lead unmistakable → add only interpreting context → quiet competing motion during the decisive gesture → let the reaction or result become the next lead.
- Variations: contrast through stillness among movement, one odd shape, scale or isolation; never by making everything bright, large and moving.
- Fix: the eye follows decoration; mute secondary motion, then restore only what improves the shot.
- Finish test: after one viewing, can someone name the action that changed the situation?

## C2 · Comparable before and after
- Use when: repair, organisation, transformation, or a contrast between methods.
- Ingredients: one property that changes; a stable subject; a matched viewpoint or layout; the intervention.
- Method: before-state → intervention → return to matched conditions → make the changed property easy to spot → show why it matters in use.
- Variations: side by side for inspection; a matched return for anticipation and continuity.
- Fix: the after-image changes light, scale, contents and angle together; hold everything but the property. Nicer presentation is not proof.
- Finish test: can the viewer point to what changed without being told which image is better?

## C3 · Detail, clue, context
- Use when: a product reveal, scale joke, mystery or visual analogy.
- Ingredients: a truthful partial view; a clear visual question; one connecting clue; the context that answers it.
- Method: open on a readable detail → add the clue that narrows the explanations → reveal the context → hold for recognition.
- Variations: reveal scale for comedy, ownership for emotion, function for explanation; one reclassification per reveal.
- Fix: the opening is an unreadable texture; give enough information to form a question. Confusion is not curiosity.
- Finish test: does the reveal explain the opening rather than replace it with another attractive image?

## C4 · Pattern and exception
- Use when: comic timing, order turning to chaos, collections, rhythmic reels.
- Ingredients: a repeated arrangement or behaviour; the expectation it sets; one meaningful exception; the resulting change.
- Method: establish the pattern → repeat until it is predictable → break it once → let the exception alter the arrangement, behaviour or outcome.
- Variations: an early exception surprises, a later one builds anticipation; repeat only as often as the pattern takes to learn.
- Fix: repetition becomes filler; vary the cost or anticipation while keeping the rule recognisable.
- Finish test: can the audience say what was expected and why the exception matters?

## C5 · Meaningful empty space
- Use when: belonging, an invitation, absence, calm, or a restrained product shot.
- Ingredients: a surrounding pattern; an identifiable absence; an action that changes what the gap means.
- Method: show what could occupy the place → leave it clearly unfilled → a character, object or offer responds to the gap → hold the new relation.
- Variations: the space can be a barrier, a missing person, breathing room or an available place; surrounding action sets the meaning.
- Fix: a blank area is assumed profound; make its role legible through a repeated arrangement or behaviour.
- Finish test: what would change, emotionally or practically, if the gap were filled?

## C6 · Change the spatial relationship
- Use when: exclusion, cooperation, conflict, trust or a power shift.
- Ingredients: two people, groups or forces; an opening arrangement; a turning point; a changed arrangement.
- Method: establish distance, hierarchy or blocked access → make the friction visible → stage the decisive action → rearrange to express the outcome.
- Variations: opposed sides to shared purpose; crowding to chosen distance; background presence to acknowledged participant. Closer is not always better.
- Fix: a reaction close-up replaces the choice; show the gesture that changes access, position or interaction.
- Finish test: would the ending read differently with the opening arrangement restored?

## C7 · Cause-to-consequence handover
- Use when: collaboration, a process chain, a journey, or a compact montage; the method behind build's motivated transitions.
- Ingredients: a motivated action; a carried object, direction, phrase or responsibility; a new context.
- Method: end the scene on an action that creates the next → carry one recognisable element across the cut → open on the consequence → continue the argument, not just the motion.
- Variations: hand over an object, a job, an invitation or an unfinished musical phrase.
- Fix: in a story or explanation, attractive shape matches hide missing causes; show what the first event causes in the second. In a poem or visual score an associative or rhythmic link may replace cause.
- Finish test: can the viewer follow the chain without a narrator saying "and then"?

## C8 · Return with a difference
- Use when: a callback, an emotional ending, a brand mnemonic or a replayable short.
- Ingredients: a memorable opening arrangement; a motif; a consequential change; a recognisable return.
- Method: establish the image innocently → develop what its elements mean → repeat enough of it to trigger recognition → change one decisive relation → leave time to register the difference.
- Variations: change who makes a gesture, who receives it, what an object is used for, or why a place is empty.
- Fix: a narrative callback erases the story's consequence; keep what mattered. In a cycle or ambient loop, a full return is the design.
- Finish test: can someone complete "it looks similar, but now it means ⟨different meaning⟩"?

## Sequence, not slideshow

For each storyboard scene, write two things beside its action: what the viewer now understands and what leads attention (one person, object or relation). Opening frames start on a situation already in progress; the turn is optional when the form has none. An explainer's frames are concept steps, a music video's are track sections, a loop's are phases of the cycle.

- Change the shot when the viewer needs a new relation, not because an interval has passed.
- Reserve contrast: a pause after busy action does more than another burst, provided the pause has a subject (recognition, consequence, invitation).
- Sequence simultaneous demands: never ask the viewer to read a long caption, parse an unfamiliar interface and catch a small reaction in the same instant.

## Re-compose for the frame shape

- **Wide**: lateral relations show opposing goals or a before-and-after together; keep decisive details close enough to catch.
- **Portrait**: use height, depth and sequential disclosure; re-stage side-by-side interactions instead of cropping one side away.
- **Both**: keep meaningful text and gestures clear of the platform's interface; build a separate portrait arrangement rather than mechanically cropping a wide one.
